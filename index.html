Scope of Machine Learning in detecting risk of Cardiovascular Diseases

With the rise of Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence over the past decade, many industries in science, particularly medical ones, have begun exploring how these fields can help advance medical imaging and detection technology. Specifically, the early detection of various diseases has become a topic of great interest in today’s times due to the massive increase in deaths from them. As such, there is constant research and deployment of deep learning models, such as neural networks, to assist in the identification of tumors from medical images or irregularities in patient reports. Through this blog, I will be focusing on health-related diseases and the use of machine learning to speed up cardiovascular disease detection while also making this accessible to the suburban regions of India. 

Introduction  

Before delving into the topic-specific complexities of machine learning, I think it is important for non-technical audiences to gain an understanding of what machine learning or Artificial Intelligence is. Then, based on that, I can explain how to use this concept practically through different models to attain robust classification predictions.

What is Machine Learning, and why is it required?

Machine learning is a branch of computer science, statistics, and a subfield of Artificial Intelligence, where we have datasets containing observations from particular databases, samples, population census, etc and we look to learn the patterns to make relevant conclusions. This involves a great amount of visual and computational components. Within these datasets, we usually have some sort of target or response that we are interested in predicting, given a new observation. Hence, this is a process that involves initial reading and exploration of the data, followed by selecting a function that could best model the identified patterns, and finally training the function on the data so that it can be used beyond this dataset. This workflow may seem very easy, and we might believe that this can easily be done by a human, so why use computers? Well, the data that we are looking at in the present day is much more complex than simple linear trends or probabilities of a certain outcome. It has become humanly impossible to even comprehend what certain data might represent as different industries are following different data storage strategies. Now, the only way to integrate all this into a fast and highly optimal solution is through using computer algorithms, particularly ones that are pre-designed to handle these tasks. 

Quick Example of Using Machine Learning

Let us discuss a quick example of using machine learning for solving a simple linear problem, and then we can proceed to discuss its implementation for disease detection (cardiovascular). Imagine we are looking to predict average house prices in Vancouver in 2026: we have data from 2000 to 2025 containing the average house prices, and it seems to have increased linearly over time (very naive assumption). Based on this, we can attempt to use a simple linear regression and manually solve for some optimal function to predict house prices for 2026. But that involves complex mathematical calculations for each parameter. Instead, we can just provide this data to an implemented (sklearn.regression) linear regression model on our computer, and learn the patterns immediately for the 25 data points. Getting a prediction is also instantaneous without having to allocate time to any manual calculations. This data was really small in comparison to real-world data, but still, we now have a good idea that machine learning saves us a lot of time and energy when predicting an unknown output.

Getting back to Cardiovascular Disease Detection

There has been a serious increase in the number of undetected cardiovascular cases, and a 2019 study by the National Institute of Health states that almost 88% individuals with undiagnosed cardiovascular disease (CVD) had visited primary or specialized somatic health care before their death. With such grave statistics, it is even more crucial that we utilize artificial intelligence to improve disease detection or create a data science interface that can take in medical report metrics and indicate the risk one may have of it. 

As mentioned previously, there have already been Neural Network implementations for detecting anomalies in medical imagery, and the research by the National Institute of Health highlights the deployment of a Logistic Regression (multivariate). Following a similar frame of idea, I would like to propose the use of Decision Trees and Logistic Regression in assisting the diagnosis of CVDs. Both these models are great options for classification-related problems, which in our case is the main concern.

Decision Trees

Root node: The starting point for the decision tree, and where our deductive algorithm begins

Child node:This is a node that follows the root and acts as the sub-threshold/condition that the algorithm must traverse to get some outcome

Branch: The line or edge connecting one node to another based on the direction of subnodes.

Leaf node:This is the final object/node in the tree model, where the outcome/prediction is defined and presented.

Decision Trees are one of the simplest machine learning models, which follow a binary-search type format. This is a deductive algorithm where we commence at some root threshold, and we can traverse the binary-search (‘yes’-‘no’) algorithm to check thresholds for child nodes until a leaf node is reached. Once this leaf node has been gained, it becomes the classification for our problem, in our problem, ‘risk of cardiovascular disease’ or ‘not at risk of cardiovascular disease’. The image below shows the general outline of a decision tree, so that the terminology used above is understood. 

How this will be used in the case of detecting cardiovascular disease is by using medical report metrics like blood sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetic (yes/no), etc. Having a training set with these metrics and targets specified, the decision tree will generate optimal thresholds from these metrics so that the algorithm can deduce whether the condition is being met or not based on the observation. We can also set the response as a binary, 1 for at ‘risk of cardiovascular disease’ and 0 for ‘not at risk of cardiovascular disease’. By the time the algorithm is terminated, we will have a prediction for each observation, with the corresponding accuracy to be calculated on how correctly the model predicted versus the actual target. The results from the decision tree when I used it for a small cardiovascular dataset from India indicated a great accuracy, which is why I propose that this model be a prime choice.

Logistic Regression

The foundational idea behind Logistic Regression is focused on how to integrate a categorical target into a regression model. We want a bounded range for our prediction, and since the classification here is binary, we can look at probabilities between 0 and 1. Now, the function for logistic regression is given as log-odds as follows,

\[\log\left(\frac{pi}{1-pi}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times X_i + \varepsilon_i\]

Here pi is the probability that the outcome is 1 (‘risk of cardiovascular disease’), and the log-odds are defined as linear with respect to the  i  parameters. One thing to note is that the natural logarithm function is monotonically increasing, so the association of the parameters with respect to the log-odds is easily interpretable. Now, this Xi is the observation we attain from the dataset, and using this observation and the appropriate algebraic manipulations, we get,

\[p_i = \frac{\exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \times X_i)}{[1 + \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \times X_i)]} \in [0, 1]\]

Under this framework, we get a proper formula for calculating the probability of the outcome being 1 (‘risk of cardiovascular disease’). This could possibly be really effective for detecting cardiovascular disease, given the calculated probability of risk. I also deployed the Logistic Regression model for the small cardiovascular dataset, and the training accuracies were relatively similar to the Decision Tree, but the validation accuracy was better (good generalization).

Conclusion: Making this accessible for everyone

The final concern is how to make the results from machine learning a proper interface, since creating an app that has this machine learning model at the backend is the aim of this project. Just understanding the machine learning part is the initial step here, but once the application is properly deployed, it can be accessed by anyone, whether they are from the city, suburbs, less developed areas, etc. This will require quite a bit of resources and funding to make possible; however, one can hope that the rise of machine learning brings a major rise in the medical industry in terms of disease detection. All in all, I believe this blog provides a detailed summary of machine learning, how to integrate it into health-related problems, and the prospects of accessibility of the application to everyone.
